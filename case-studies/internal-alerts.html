Planning emergency alerts before the emergency

Before the Washington Metropolitan Area Transit Authority (WMATA) chose a system to alert its own staff in a life-threatening emergency, I studied four real emergencies so the requirements came from what actually goes wrong, not from vendor feature lists.

Role
Management consultant and researcher, Ignyte Group. Designed and ran requirements workshops with the client lead.
Timeline
June 2018 to spring 2019
Methods
Secondary research (after-action reports), workshop design, requirements, process mapping, message design
Outcome
42 draft requirements, an active attacker alert process, and a plan to expand a system staff already knew

The problem

WMATA already sent plenty of alerts. Riders got them, Metro Transit Police (MTPD) sent their own, and messages ran on screens across stations and buses. But none of them were built for one specific kind of problem: an emergency that many WMATA employees needed to know about and act on, and the public didn't.

The real problem was the chaos of uncertainty in communications. You just didn't know every time how people would get the message. Many frontline employees couldn't carry personal phones at work, so the most obvious way to reach someone in a crisis often wasn't there at all.

The Federal Transit Administration (FTA) required WMATA to fix this through a corrective action plan.

What I did

I picked real emergencies on purpose. I lived in England as a kid, and school field trips took us into London. Seeing the 2005 bombings bring down the Tube for weeks in a place I knew was terrifying. The Navy Yard shooting happened the year before I decided to move to DC, and it broke my heart to see the suffering. After I moved, I thought everything was safe and clean, and I quickly realized how much both were hanging in the balance every single day.

I researched four emergencies through their official after-action reports: the 2005 London bombings, the 2013 Navy Yard shooting, the 2007 Virginia Tech shooting, and Super Bowl XLVIII, a huge event where communications went right. I sorted every lesson into the phase of an alert where it belonged.

I built the workshop deck around those stories, and my team wrote the discussion questions. After we first walked the client through it, they cut the Super Bowl case. It had far fewer lessons WMATA could use than the other three. Navy Yard is where the room got down to business: many people there had their own connection to it, and they could picture their own frontline staff in that building without their phones.

Then I turned the lessons into a process. For an active attacker, the most urgent case, I built the flow from the first call to the all-clear and wrote the messages for each step. Every major choice was deliberate, and each one closes a gap from the research:

I wrote every message three ways, because one wording doesn't fit every channel: a spoken version for phone calls and public address, a short one for text and the app, and a full one for email and screens.

Building the process also exposed a gap underneath it. For an alert to reach people by where they work, and where they'd report to if systems or buildings had to close, the HR system needed accurate contact and location data for everyone. Contractors weren't in that system at all. We flagged it as something WMATA had to fix before any alert system could work, whichever one they chose.

What we learned

The biggest lesson was that an emergency alert succeeds or fails long before anyone hits send. Most lessons were about planning, not sending. Of the 16 lessons from the four emergencies, 7 landed in planning. Only 2 were about sending the message itself.

That became one of the big pillars of how we framed the requirements. Instead of a list of features for blasting out messages, the 42 draft requirements covered the whole lifecycle: templates written and approved ahead of time, clear authority to send, accurate contact lists, and a way to confirm people were safe afterward. Only 7 were about sending.

It also reframed the chaos of uncertainty. You just didn't know every time how people would get the message. The answer was all the work and infrastructure that goes into planning, organizing, and managing an event, and seeing that work as a cycle, not a single moment. People needed the systems and the rules set up long before an event happened, to maximize safety and minimize risk. Nobody said it in one sentence. We all got there in pieces.

What changed

By early 2019, the work had turned into three things WMATA could act on:

  • 42 draft requirements covering the whole alert lifecycle, not just sending
  • A process for the most urgent case, an active attacker, walked through in workshops, with ready-to-send messages for every stage, from the first alert to the all-clear
  • A recommendation to expand an existing system that people were already familiar with across the whole organization, instead of buying a new one. Replacing it would have meant paying for the same capability twice.

Between the first and final versions of the process, WMATA moved sending authority to Metro Transit Police. In spring 2019, I moved to another WMATA project before rollout began.

What I'd do differently

I would have held more workshops with the people who actually receive alerts, all along the chain. We didn't have the time or funding to do it. The leaders in our workshops knew the potential issues inside and out, but we were seeing the work from the airplane in the sky, not from the ground on the tarmac.

Lower-level managers, closer to the work, could have helped us check every angle and catch the requirements we missed. I think they'd have brought a different level of understanding, and a different level of ownership too.
