Higher Logic · SUS benchmarking

Building Higher Logic's first usability baseline across three products

A three-phase System Usability Scale (SUS) benchmarking program, designed from scratch to replace anecdote-driven quality conversations with a shared, cross-product measure of usability.

Sole UX researcher
Timeline

Products
Real Magnet, Informz, and Higher Logic Thrive
Methods

So what

Higher Logic was making big product decisions, consolidating two legacy marketing platforms and modernizing its flagship community product before expanding AI into all products, with no usability data to inform either. As the company's first UX researcher, I built the measurement infrastructure from nothing: three studies, three products, a mixed-methods framework that paired defensible scores with qualitative depth, and a path from findings to the customers who needed outreach most. The result was a shared language for quality that didn't exist before I got there.

Background

The problem: no shared language for quality

When I joined Higher Logic in 2021 as the company's first-ever UX researcher, the product team faced a strategic challenge with no data to answer it: before investing in modernizing or consolidating its portfolio, leadership needed to understand where each product actually stood with users.

Higher Logic had three core products: Real Magnet and Informz, two legacy marketing automation platforms that needed honest evaluation before any consolidation decisions, and Higher Logic Thrive, the flagship online community platform being primed for AI features. The marketing automation products needed assessment to understand what was salvageable. Thrive needed a current-state baseline before new complexity got layered on top.

Quality conversations were driven by whoever complained loudest: support tickets, escalations from customer success managers (CSMs), and anecdotes from sales calls. There was no consistent measurement and no way to separate signal from noise. SUS studies had never been run at Higher Logic. I ran the first one on Real Magnet, then pushed to run the same study on Informz and Thrive, so each product had a baseline before anything changed.

Research design

The approach: SUS plus qualitative context

I used the System Usability Scale (SUS), a validated 10-item questionnaire, as the quantitative backbone of each study. SUS produces a score from 0 to 100 that maps to an industry-standard grading scale, which makes it ideal for benchmarking and comparison across products.

I paired it with five open-ended questions and thematic coding of every written response. The mixed-methods design gave leadership both a defensible number and a clear explanation of what was driving it, in the same deliverable.

Five open-ended questions

  • Q1

    Rating explanation

    Please explain why you ranked the questions the way you did.

  • Q2

    Frustrations

    What, if anything, is currently frustrating for you in [product]?

  • Q3

    Missing features

    What functionality is currently missing?

  • Q4

    What's working

    Please describe anything working well that we should not change.

  • Q5

    Anything else

    Please share anything else about your experience that you want us to know.

Execution

Three studies, three products, three and a half years

I ran each study in sequence, with recruitment and framing tailored to a specific business question. Surveys went out through SurveyMonkey, and I coded responses in structured spreadsheets, then synthesized them into executive presentations with supporting data.

  1. December 2021

    Real Magnet

    Higher Logic's oldest marketing automation product, built in the mid-2000s and deeply embedded in long-tenured customer workflows. The strategic question: what's salvageable, and what's broken beyond repair? I recruited through direct customer outreach. When scores came back below benchmark, leadership wasn't surprised; the technical debt was well known. What they'd never had was a prioritized, structured picture of exactly where the problems lived.

  2. October 2022

    Informz

    The more actively used of the two marketing automation platforms. The strategic question: how does it compare to Real Magnet, and what would it take to modernize it into a combined offering? I expanded recruitment to three channels: direct email, CSM touchpoints, and an in-app prompt through Pendo, which gave us the largest sample of the three studies. Pain was concentrated in email campaign workflows, inconsistent folder structures, and fragmented reporting. When scores came back below benchmark, leadership was more surprised than they'd been with Real Magnet. I worked with my manager to identify quick wins the development team could ship right away, then closed the loop by telling participants what had changed, which built a reusable research panel in the process.

  3. May 2025

    Higher Logic Thrive

    By 2025, Thrive had become the centerpiece of Higher Logic's strategy, and leadership was preparing to accelerate AI features across the platform. The strategic question: before integrating AI and consolidating products into Thrive, what do customers actually think of it right now? I recruited through an in-app prompt and a post in the Higher Logic User Group community, and nearly everyone who took the survey also answered the open-ended questions. I presented the findings and recommendations to leadership.

Results

The numbers: below benchmark across the board

All three products scored below the industry average of 68. Real Magnet confirmed leadership's instinct about accumulated usability debt. Informz came closest to the benchmark. Thrive, the flagship, scored 52.4, in the 15th percentile, right before an accelerated AI rollout. That was a direct challenge to the AI expansion timeline.

Deep dive: Thrive study, May 2025

What the data revealed

Coding the written responses surfaced four dominant themes that cut across admins, marketers, end users, and implementers. That pointed to systemic friction, not role-specific friction, and the patterns were consistent enough to drive prioritized recommendations.

Theme 1: Reporting is a major bottleneck

The most frequently raised concern among low and middle scorers alike. People described analytics split between message-level and platform-level views, stitching exports together by hand, and needing outside tools, or paying for custom reports, to get insights they expected to be built in. Roles affected: admins, marketers, leadership.

Theme 2: Permissions and admin roles create bottlenecks

Many teams granted Super Admin access to only a few people because of risk concerns, but that blocked everyday tasks like reporting, editing templates, and exporting data. People also noticed permissions working differently from one area of the product to the next. Roles affected: community managers, content editors.

Theme 3: An outdated interface that's hard to navigate

People often described the interface as dated, clunky, and hard to find their way around, especially compared with tools like HubSpot or Mailchimp. Even longtime users said they still got lost in the menus. The feeling held across user types and tenure. Areas affected: navigation, email builder, campaign setup.

Theme 4: Email problems cause people to drop off

Email formatting, list management, and unsubscribe controls felt incomplete or impossible to customize. Formatting broke when copied from templates, and an unsubscribe page that couldn't be customized led some members to drop all communications. Because email is central to community and campaign work, these issues had an outsized effect on how people felt about the whole platform. Areas affected: templates, delivery, list management.

Bright spot: support. Across every score level, the Support team was the most consistently praised part of the Thrive experience, and the "Ask the Advisor" service drew unprompted praise from several people. That was a genuine trust asset, and a strategic entry point for onboarding and adoption programs.

Key insight: People who wrote about email workflows had the lowest average scores of any theme group. This wasn't just a feature frustration. It was a trust problem that dragged down how people saw the whole platform.

Participant personas: Thrive study

Who was using Thrive, and how they experienced it

Four consistent user types emerged from the coding, each running into friction in distinct but often overlapping ways.

  • Super Admins

    Role restrictions funnel every task through them. They can't delegate to Community Admins, so requests pile up in their queue.

  • Marketers

    Inflexible email formatting, reporting too fragmented to be useful, and a confusing unsubscribe workflow that limits control over member communications. They want to run a campaign and measure it, start to finish, in one place.

  • End users

    Confusing navigation, a dated interface, and trouble finding content or understanding what the platform offers them.

  • Implementers

    Complex integrations with association management systems (AMS) and customer relationship management (CRM) tools, inconsistent setup guidance, and support knowledge gaps across connected systems.

Recommendations: Thrive study

What I recommended

Five prioritized recommendations, based on how often each theme came up, how many people it affected, and what it meant for the business.

  1. Unify reporting

    Bring message-level and platform-level analytics into a single interface. This addresses the most-cited pain point across all user types, saves staff time, and makes outcomes easier to see.

  2. Make permissions more granular

    Add finer role-based permissions so Community Admins can handle routine tasks without escalating to a Super Admin. This empowers teams and reduces the support burden.

  3. Modernize navigation

    Overhaul the menu structure and layout so things are easier to find. Consistent sidebar navigation, pinned content, and smart filtering were the most-requested specific improvements across all user types.

  4. Make email templates more flexible

    Fix formatting breakage, slow email builder load times, and the inability to customize unsubscribe flows. These issues hit the lowest-scoring participants hardest.

  5. Surface support resources earlier

    Promote "Ask the Advisor" during onboarding, before people hit frustration points, and lean on the Support team's strong reputation to build trust and keep customers.

Impact

From data to decisions

What the research changed

  • Established Higher Logic's first-ever systematic usability baseline across its product portfolio, replacing anecdote-driven quality conversations with a shared measurement framework
  • Real Magnet and Informz findings directly informed which marketing automation features to preserve, consolidate, or rebuild, turning complaints into prioritized roadmap input
  • Delivered quick-win improvements from the Informz study that the engineering team could ship without waiting for a full planning cycle
  • Connected at-risk customers with their CSM: participants who opted into follow-up contact and scored lowest were routed for proactive outreach
  • Closed the feedback loop with Informz participants, showing that taking part in research led to visible changes, and building an engaged panel for future studies
  • Placed the flagship platform in the 15th percentile for usability ahead of a planned AI rollout, a data-backed argument for fixing the foundation before adding new complexity
  • Showed that usability debt was systemic across all three products, reframing quality from isolated complaints into a cross-product priority
What happened next

The rerun that didn't happen

Each baseline was meant to be measured again after changes shipped, so we could see what actually improved. When there wasn't budget for the rerun, I proposed asking again in six months. The rerun I planned would have paired SUS with one neutral question: "How have the AI features changed your experience?" on a scale from much worse to much better.

My time at Higher Logic ended shortly after I presented the Thrive findings, before that check-in came. The baselines are still there for whoever picks this up next.

Reflection

What I learned

What worked well

  • Mixed-methods design gave quantitative credibility and qualitative depth in the same deliverable
  • Using the same SUS method every time made fair comparisons across products and against the industry possible
  • Recruiting through several channels for Informz improved sample quality and representativeness
  • Thematic coding created reusable frameworks, so each study built on the last
  • Routing opted-in, low-scoring participants to Customer Success created business value beyond the research itself
  • Closing the feedback loop built an engaged participant panel

What I'd do differently

  • Agree with stakeholders up front on which score thresholds would trigger which decisions, before fielding, not after
  • Run studies on a fixed schedule from the start, with the reruns budgeted, so tracking over time doesn't depend on later approval
  • Segment scores by user tenure and role from day one rather than after the fact
  • Build a lighter quarterly pulse survey for tracking between full studies
